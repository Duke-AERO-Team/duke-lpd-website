## The redesign

Prometheus is Duke AERO Liquid Propulsion's second bipropellant liquid engine,
and a two-year project built around short design-build-test cycles rather than
one long development arc. Every component is designed against flight
requirements rather than stand requirements. The goal is a vehicle stack for the
FAR-OUT 2028 challenge: a test launch in spring 2028 to a target apogee of
14,000 ft, the ceiling at the Bayboro launch site.

The engine burns nitrous oxide and isopropyl alcohol. The nitrous is
self-pressurizing, fed from its own vapor pressure. The IPA is fed by an
electric pump the team is designing in-house, with a nitrous vapor bleed line
holding the IPA tank at the pressure the pump needs at its inlet. At take-off
the engine makes 3,650 N of thrust and burns for about five and a half seconds.
Thrust tapers toward 3,000 N by burnout as the nitrous pressure decays.

The flight vehicle is 6.125 in. in diameter, about 11 ft long, and around 105 lb
fully fueled. It has a carbon-fiber airframe and fins, and a fiberglass nose
cone so the radios can see out.

## What changed from Eno

Eno ran kerosene and nitrous, pressure-fed, through a bolted clamshell chamber.
Prometheus changes the fuel, puts a pump on the fuel side, and steps chamber
pressure up from Eno's 350 psi to 400 psi. Eno hardware still carries forward.
The mass budget starts from Eno's preliminary flight stack, the thrust plate on
the new stand is repurposed Eno hardware, and the valve servos are sized from
the actuation torque Eno's valves needed.

## The engine

Mixture ratio is set at 2.6, well below the roughly 5 that gives peak specific
impulse at 400 psi. That keeps flame temperature down and leaves enough IPA
flowing to cool a regenerative chamber. Total propellant flow is 1.73 kg/s, and
the nozzle is sized to expand to the pressure at 1,000 ft.

<Figure
  src={ispCurve}
  alt="Line plot of ambient specific impulse against mixture ratio at a chamber pressure of 400 psia, with a separate curve for each nozzle expansion ratio between 3.3 and 5.8."
  caption="Ambient specific impulse against mixture ratio at 400 psia, swept across expansion ratios — the trade behind the chamber and nozzle."
/>

The injector is a pintle, chosen for atomization and because it's easy to make
and change. A stainless steel faceplate sits under an aluminum manifold plate
carrying the propellant inlets and three ports for pressure transducers. The
whole manifold comes in under 2 lb. The copper pintle tip sprays IPA out through
radial slots into a sheet of nitrous coming down the annulus around it. The tip
is made in two pieces so the slots can be cut with a slitting saw.

There are two chambers. A regeneratively cooled chamber for flight is still in
design: a 3D-printed aluminum liner with its cooling channels built in, wrapped
in a carbon-fiber structural jacket. The one built first is a heavy "battleship"
ablative chamber for testing. Its cast phenolic liner wears away through each
burn and is replaced between firings, with a graphite nozzle, so the stand can
run up to three hot fires a day.

<Figure
  src={ablativeChamber}
  alt="CAD cross-section of the ablative test chamber: the injector manifold at the top, a thick liner shown in tan lining the chamber, and a graphite nozzle converging and expanding at the bottom."
  caption="The ablative test chamber in section: injector, liner, and graphite nozzle."
/>

The IPA pump needs to put out 550 psi. At a small impeller and a modest flow
rate that means 20,000–30,000 rpm, which is too low a specific speed for a
conventional impeller. So it's a Barske pump: straight radial blades in a
concentric volute, simple enough to machine in-house and iterate on. For testing
it's driven by an off-the-shelf RC-class brushless motor and motor controller on
its own 6S battery, chosen for margin rather than mass. The flight hardware will
be reselected once testing has measured current draw and efficiency.

## Flight tanks and feed system

The tanks are the part of Prometheus furthest along into hardware. Both are
6061-T6 aluminum tubes rated for 900 psi. They share one closure design: a 2:1
elliptical head, designed to the ASME pressure-vessel code. The nitrous tank
carries 7.72 kg for the burn. The IPA tank carries 2.87 kg behind a domed
piston. The first closures are already turned. The one in this page's banner
came off the lathe in the shop.

Flow is switched by servo-driven ball valves, a ½ in. main fuel valve and a ¾
in. main oxidizer valve. A ¼ in. valve on the bleed line throttles 900 psi
nitrous vapor down to hold the IPA tank near 325 psi. That one is already built
and has been tested.

## Avionics and ground control

Every earlier cold flow ran through a wired setup: a mess of cables, a handful
of protoboards, an Arduino UNO, and an external DAQ, all driven from a laptop
running LabVIEW over a long USB run. It worked, but it cost hours of wiring and
troubleshooting before each test, and a liquid engine doesn't forgive a flaky
connection the way a bench experiment does. The division's control boards
started as the answer to that pile of wires, and TITAN is where they've led.

### Control board V1.0

V1.0 folds sensing, actuation, and power into a single board built around an
ESP32-C3, talking to a laptop over WiFi instead of a USB tether — the same link
carries telemetry out and servo commands in, so the DAQ and its cabling come out
of the test setup entirely.

Sensing runs through three 8-channel TLA2518 ADCs sampled over SPI, with enough
of their combined 24 channels broken out to give the board its twelve-channel
pressure-transducer interface, each channel hardware-averaged to cut noise
before it ever reaches software. Actuation runs through a PCA9685 PWM driver,
offloading servo timing from the MCU entirely and giving the board's
eight-channel servo bus 12-bit resolution on every valve. Separate buck
converters keep servo power and logic power on their own rails off the same 12 V
input, and every field connection — PTs, servos, power — lands on a keyed JST
connector, so a cable can't go in backwards during a rushed pre-test hookup.

### Ground control

The board pairs with a Python ground-station application that gives the operator
a command console, live per-channel servo state, and a real-time plot of all
twelve pressure channels running through a configurable moving average. Valve
actuation can be driven live from the console or from a timed sequence script,
so an abort or a firing sequence runs the same way every time instead of
depending on an operator's timing. Every run — telemetry and actuator state
alike — logs to CSV for teardown after the test.

### What cold flow found

Running V1.0 through cold flow cut electrical setup from hours to minutes and
more than doubled pressure-transducer polling, from 40 Hz to 100 Hz, once
sampling moved onto dedicated hardware instead of a DAQ. Testing also found the
board's limits: servo current capacity was undersized for sustained actuation,
and analog routing on a single board left the pressure readings more susceptible
to noise than the team wanted. Both are the reason there's a V2.0.

### Board V2.0

V2.0 splits the single board in two — a control board carrying the MCU and servo
bus, and a separate sampling board carrying pressure, thermocouple, and
load-cell inputs — so switching noise from servo drive never shares a board with
the analog signals it would otherwise corrupt. The MCU moves to an ESP32-S3 with
onboard LoRa and local data logging, adding a wireless fallback and an on-board
record independent of the ground-station link. Sampling gains thermocouple
channels through an AD8495 amplifier and load-cell channels through an HX711
ADC, and servo power moves to a higher-current buck converter sized for the draw
V1.0 undersized. The two-board stack is built and is now being brought up ahead
of its first cold flow.

### TITAN flight computer

TITAN is the board that flies, and the same board runs the ground side too. One
TITAN serves as the vehicle management system on the rocket and another as the
ground management system, differing only in firmware role. Each is built around
an STM32F411 BlackPill. A LoRa and 2.4 GHz BLE radio module provides LoRa
telemetry down after launch and BLE to the ground system on the pad. A CAN link
drives the electropump's motor controller. Three BDPI ports carry data and power
to the peripheral boards over a single RJ45 cable each. BDPI is the team's own
spec.

The firmware has one rule: on the rocket, TITAN is the only thing that owns
vehicle state and authorizes outputs. Every cycle, at 20 Hz or faster, it reads
tank pressures and temperatures, pump speed, and valve position. It updates the
flight state, commands the actuator board and the pump to hold IPA tank
pressure, sends telemetry down, and writes the full state to onboard flash.
Either computer can call an abort on its own. TITAN watches for stale data,
out-of-limit pressures or temperatures, pump faults, valves that aren't where
they were told to be, and lost links. Both run on Zephyr RTOS.

## Ground system

The test setup is built to run three tests a day. Between tests, only the deluge
water needs refilling. The vehicle stack rides a vertical rail that pivots down
to horizontal for loading, and fires into a thrust stand that measures thrust
through a load cell. A ground fill station holds the nitrogen, nitrous, and IPA
supply tanks, with a swing arm that carries the fill lines to the vehicle. At
the end of fill, servo-driven winches pull the team's own quick disconnects off
and swing the arm clear.

Fill is scripted down to the valve. IPA goes in first, under nitrogen pressure.
Then nitrous fills until a camera sees liquid coming out of a vent dip tube,
which marks the right fill level. Heating pads then bring the nitrous up to 900
psi over about ten minutes. The sequence ends in a built-in hold, so a slow soak
delays T-0 rather than shifting it. In the last two seconds the pump spins up,
the igniter lights, the deluge opens, and the main valves open, IPA a
quarter-second ahead of the nitrous.

## Where Prometheus stands today

Prometheus has been through its critical design review. The avionics boards are
designed, and the first tank closures are coming off the lathe. Packaging
everything into the 6 in. airframe is out of scope for this academic year. The
work now is proving the system on the ground, in cold flow and then hot fire on
the stand. The photographs below are from the shop this term.
